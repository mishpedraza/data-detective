# Discover investigation audit

This audit covers the nine investigations linked from Discover. Ratings describe the original explanations. The questions, lead figures, datasets, quiz and navigation stay in place. The figures for permits and fertility illustrate narrower comparisons than their investigation titles. The edits explain that scope.

## Are houses really more expensive than they used to be?

**Needs improvement.** The main chart compares Spain's national real house-price index in 2007 and 2025. The OECD endpoint comparison is approximately 164.1 to 143.0, a 13% drop after rounding. The natural question is why buying can still feel harder. The old mortgage example was plausible but left readers to connect the national benchmark to Spain's post-2014 recovery and local supply constraints.

**Recommendation.** Explain how both comparisons can be true. Prices recovered from the post-crash trough while remaining below the 2007 national benchmark. Banco de España describes household demand outpacing new supply, with uneven pressure across places. Access to financing, the deposit and local prices decide what that benchmark means for a particular buyer. The national chart cannot establish how a first-time buyer's burden changed. That would require local prices, buyer incomes, loan terms and deposits measured together.

**Sources.** [OECD annual real house-price series for Spain](https://sdmx.oecd.org/public/rest/v1/data/OECD.ECO.MPD,DSD_AN_HOUSE_PRICES@DF_HOUSE_PRICES,1.0/ESP.A.RHP.?startPeriod=2007&endPeriod=2025&format=csvfile) · [Banco de España, 2023 Annual Report, Chapter 4 and charts](https://www.bde.es/wbe/en/publicaciones/informes-memorias-anuales/informe-anual/informe-anual-2023.html).

## Does having children make people happier?

**Strong.** The German panel timeline shows change from the same people's earlier happiness. Estimated differences are +0.30 a year before birth, +0.32 during the birth year and +0.05 one to two years afterward. Why does the rise start before birth and then fade? The previous explanation identified anticipation and partnership transitions but did not distinguish what the authors tested from what they suggested.

**Recommendation.** Explain that allowing for changes such as partnership, income and work made relatively little difference to the longer-run pattern. The authors suggest anticipation and partnership may contribute to the earlier rise. The panel cannot separate those influences or tell us how the same people would have felt had they remained childless. The plotted coefficients were checked against the openly available working-paper table accompanying the published study.

**Source.** [Myrskylä & Margolis, Table 2 and sensitivity analyses](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13524-014-0321-x).

## Are we actually working harder than our grandparents?

**Needs improvement.** The main figure shows that annual hours per employed person fell by about 355 in Spain, 142 in the US and 637 in Germany between 1970 and 2023. All six endpoints were checked against the original workbook's Data sheet, variable avh. Why did hours fall more in Spain than the US? The existing US time-diary paragraph changed the subject to unpaid work without first addressing this contrast.

**Recommendation.** Explain that the averages measure how much work is done per worker over a year. A change in full-time schedules, the mix of part-time work or weeks worked can move the average. The endpoints cannot tell us which accounted for Spain's larger fall. Keep the US time diaries as separate evidence that lower work hours and more leisure can come through different channels. Explaining the gap needs harmonised country and period data breaking annual hours into schedules, weeks and job composition.

**Sources.** [Penn World Table 11.0 workbook](https://doi.org/10.34894/FABVLR) · [Aguiar & Hurst, US time diaries](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12082).

## Does immigration really increase crime?

**Strong for the charted permit comparison.** The Italian cutoff study estimates a 0.6 percentage-point reduction from a 1.1% baseline for applicants close to the cutoff. What changed when an applicant received a permit, and what does this say about immigration more broadly? The old copy suggested legal work and mentioned UK results, but compressed the difference between getting a permit and increasing immigration.

**Recommendation.** Explain the near-cutoff comparison, and present access to legal work as one plausible channel. Separate the broader review's very weak average association from the narrower permit estimate. The studies leave the size of the work channel and the effect on Spanish homicide open. The original Pinotti abstract supports the effect size. Full text of the UK study and the meta-analysis were not independently inspected for this pass. The revised explanation avoids adding specific findings from the UK, and retains the comparison studies in the research panel.

**Sources.** [Pinotti, study abstract](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20150355) · [Ousey & Kubrin, review](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-criminol-032317-092026).

## Why do women earn less despite outperforming men in education?

**Needs improvement.** The Danish chart estimates a roughly 20% long-run gross earnings penalty for mothers around a first birth, with little change for fathers. Why does that happen even when women have qualifications? The old explanation moved quickly to scheduling premiums without saying how much the paper attributes to whether someone works, their hours and their hourly pay.

**Recommendation.** Emphasise that the three margins were roughly equally important. Goldin's analysis of pay premiums for long or particular hours offers context for one margin, rather than measuring its contribution in Denmark. The Danish study does not explain the whole European pay gap or track the same people as the separate Eurostat education and wage summaries. Those Eurostat statistics are retained as historical context. Their source landing page was checked, but the historical table values were not independently reproduced.

**Sources.** [Kleven, Landais & Søgaard, Figure 3 and discussion](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20180010) · [Goldin, published abstract](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.104.4.1091) · [Eurostat gender statistics](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Gender_statistics).

## Is the world becoming more dangerous?

**Strong for the stated conflict measure.** The figure breaks down the roughly 122,000 battle deaths in state-based conflicts in 2023. The accompanying data report 59 conflicts and a death toll less than half the previous year's, still the third-highest since 1989. How can more conflicts produce fewer deaths? The old explanation pointed to Ethiopia but did not name the end of the Tigray war or show how concentrated the remaining toll was.

**Recommendation.** Name the shift out of Ethiopia and put Ukraine and Israel–Palestine together at roughly three quarters of the remaining 2023 toll, using PRIO's rounded counts. The conflict count treats each conflict as one. The death count reflects intensity. Neither is a comprehensive measure of safety. The WHO child mortality figure is separate and sourced as such.

**Sources.** [PRIO, executive summary and Figure 6](https://www.prio.org/publications/14006) · [WHO, under-five mortality](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/children-reducing-mortality).

## Are younger generations poorer than their parents?

**Strong for the age-matched accounting comparison.** The Federal Reserve's Table 4 shows roughly $176,300 versus $173,200 in total assets for millennials and boomers, and roughly $84,600 versus $59,300 in debts. Why does similar ownership leave younger households with less wealth? The old explanation answered the accounting question, but the totals hide a second comparison involving homeownership and Generation X.

**Recommendation.** Explain that millennials' debt exceeded boomers' by about $25,000 while assets differed by around $3,000. Note that homeownership was 34% for millennials compared with 48% for boomers in the sample, and weaker asset holdings account for more of the gap with Gen X. The table cannot isolate why these differences arose or establish whether later cohorts follow the pattern. Keep these snapshots anchored in 1989, 2001 and 2016 and in means, which may differ from the experience of a typical household.

**Source.** [Kurz, Li & Vine, Survey of Consumer Finances, Table 4](https://doi.org/10.17016/feds.2018.080).

## Is crime really rising as much as people think?

**Needs improvement.** The chart juxtaposes Pew's tally of 23 out of 27 Gallup polls with the FBI's 49% decline in recorded violent crime from 1993 to 2022. Does that mean poll respondents were wrong? The old explanation noted different time windows but did not anchor the local/national point in the actual paired poll comparison.

**Recommendation.** Give the October 2023 Gallup comparison of 77% nationally versus 55% locally. Explain why the long-run line cannot grade a one-year opinion about all crime. A real increase such as the spike in murder in 2020 can occur inside a decades-long decline. Testing accuracy needs aligned geography, offence definitions and dates, with victimisation data where available. Neither source establishes why individual respondents answered as they did.

**Sources.** [Pew's FBI and Gallup comparisons](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/04/24/what-the-data-says-about-crime-in-the-us/) · [Gallup October 2023 survey](https://news.gallup.com/poll/544442/americans-crime-problem-serious.aspx).

## Why are wealthy countries having fewer children?

**Needs improvement for the comparison, needs research for causal attribution.** The lead chart shows approximately 1.9 births per woman in France and 1.4 in Germany in 2000. Figure 8b plots the OECD rates and broadly similar GDP per person. Why did the two places differ? The old explanation named generic work-family trade-offs without showing which mattered in these countries.

**Recommendation.** Describe the contrast documented around that period. French crèches and nursery schools helped parents combine paid work and care. Fagnani's direct comparison describes scarce toddler care and stronger expectations of care at home in West Germany. Dorbritz documents the different legacy of public care in eastern Germany. Use those findings to explain why similar national incomes can coexist with different work-family conditions. These comparisons cannot assign how much of the national gap came from childcare, leave, work schedules or preferences. Estimating that would need comparable data by region, age, and birth order, and a way to separate actual policy changes from simultaneous social shifts. Annual fertility rates also reflect the timing of births.

**Sources.** [Doepke et al., Figure 8b, original chart of rounded values](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29948) · [OECD fertility series credited in Figure 8](https://doi.org/10.1787/8272fb01-en) · [Fagnani, verified abstract, France versus West Germany](https://doi.org/10.1080/1366880022010218) · [Toulemon, Pailhé & Rossier, France, section 5.2](https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol19/16/) · [Dorbritz, Germany, sections 2 and 6](https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol19/17/).

## Access and verification notes

The audit checked the lead chart and each public explanation against its own source. For housing and working hours, the original OECD API series and Penn World Table workbook reproduced the app's values. For the other figures, the cited paper or report was checked where accessible. For the fertility chart, the rounded values are sourced to the printed Figure 8b and the OECD series it names. The audit did not reproduce that historical OECD snapshot from a fresh export.

The outdated Eurostat crime metadata link returned 404 and was replaced with Eurostat's working crime statistics and comparability page. Some publisher links rejected automated access. The audit used accessible working papers, publisher abstracts, or indexed abstracts where available. It does not certify the full publisher text of Fagnani, Ousey and Kubrin, Bell and colleagues, or Alonso-Borrego and colleagues. The Fagnani abstract explicitly describes the childcare and work patterns used in the new fertility explanation.
